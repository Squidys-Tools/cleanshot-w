Notes on Attention, Winter 2026

by Theo Wren

Somewhere in the middle of last year I started keeping every article I meant to read. I did not read them, of course. I collected them the way a crow collects keys: compulsively, proudly, and entirely without purpose. My bookmarks folder became a small city of good intentions, and I was its only resident who never showed up for work.

This essay is an attempt to understand that city. I want to know why we save without organizing, why a wall of saved things feels like a promise, and whether there is a version of archiving that is closer to care than to hoarding.

The myth of the empty notebook

There is a specific joy in buying a notebook that already seems to contain the future. The blank pages are a kind of optimism you can hold. I think software mimics this. When a capture tool says "save anything, find it later," it is selling you the same feeling: a small white page that will eventually become your mind.

The trouble begins when the page never fills because it fills too easily. Every tab, every screenshot, every half-remembered PDF gets poured in without a label, and the notebook stops being a mind and becomes a landfill with search.

Search as the new shelf

We were taught that organization is a shelf: folders, categories, a place for everything. But shelves require a decision at the moment of capture, and the moment of capture is precisely when we least want to decide. A library that searches itself removes that friction. It asks nothing of you at the door and answers questions later, when you actually have one.

The risk is that search becomes a crutch and the archive becomes a void you can never fully know. You do not browse your saved things; you interrogate them. And so the archive slowly stops being a place you visit and starts being a file you file away.

Rediscovery as care

The best thing a personal library can do is show you something you had forgotten. Not because an algorithm calculated your taste, but because the thing was good once and is probably still good. Serendipity is not a gimmick; it is the entire argument for saving at all. If you never look back, the archive is just a tax you pay on the present.

I have started opening my old captures the way people open shoeboxes of letters. It takes discipline. The interface offers you a search bar and you have to refuse it, just once, and wander instead. That refusal is the whole practice. Save without organizing is only half the slogan; the other half is look back on purpose.

What the archive is for

After a winter of experiments, I have a working theory. The archive is not a warehouse and it is not a mirror. It is a garden you tend by walking through it. The plants are the things you cared about, the weeds are the things you never will read, and the paths are the searches that lead you back to what mattered.

I still save too much. I still bookmark articles I will never open and screenshot quotes I will never quote. But I have stopped feeling guilty about it, because the collection itself is a record of attention. It tells you what you were curious about in a given year, and curiosity, it turns out, is the only thing the archive is really preserving.
